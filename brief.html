Cascade through downstream chains

Wave 3 tail · multi-quarter
  • Bunker fuel Type 4 Distribution cascade confirmed; no new formal FM filed (Tier 1): Bunker fuel in Singapore went up to more than $800 ($725 per U.S. ton) as of early May (AP 12 May, Tier 2). Ship & Bunker: $846/mt SGX VLSFO 11 May (Tier 1). Shortage of bunker fuel will translate to higher shipping costs; that will be an issue first in Asia, which relies heavily on Middle Eastern oil; in Singapore, the world's biggest refueling hub for bunker fuel, reserves are dwindling and prices are spiking (AP 12 May, Tier 2). Shipping companies are absorbing the brunt of the costs for now, said June Goh, an oil analyst for market intelligence firm Sparta Commodities, but this may soon "pass on to the customers" (Tier 2).
  • Aluminium contract cascade locked in; 12-month EGA rebuild duration (Tier 1): EGA Al Taweelah smelter offline since 29 March; complete restoration could take up to 12 months (Tier 1, EGA statement 3 April). Buyers locked into contract renegotiation and alternative sourcing for 2–3 quarters minimum. No acceleration reported 16–19 May.
  • Aviation sector (Lufthansa, KLM, Qatar Airways) route suspensions ongoing (Tier 2): No new route closures filed 16–19 May, but existing suspension list (extended through mid-June per EASA CZIB updates 12 May) continues. Aerospace supply-chain implications (engine overhauls, spare parts routing via Doha) remain locked in multi-month timelines.
Why this matters — Wave 3 cascade (physical absence / downstream feedstock starvation) is now embedded in multi-quarter timelines rather than acute events. Bunker fuel scarcity in Singapore (the global hub) is forcing shipping cost pass-through to downstream logistics, increasing consumer prices for goods and services first in Asia, then globally. EGA's 12-month rebuild means aluminium supply constraints persist through Q1 2027. No new formal operator FMs filed, but the impact is cascading through cost increases and delivery delays rather than supply interruptions.
Implication — The cascade is now structural, not episodic. Procurement teams are absorbing bunker premiums, negotiating force-majeure-like cost adjustments without formal FM declarations, and extending lead times. The risk: one new Hard FM in any major operator (maritime, petrochemical, or power) could trigger rapid re-cascade as buyers and shippers suddenly perceive the supply chain as unreliable beyond the current price premium. The boundary condition for L5 (regime collapse) remains: maritime operator Type 4 bunker FM OR KPC/SABIC extend restart timelines past 20 May. Neither triggered as of 19 May.

Substitution / alternative sourcing

Bifurcation emerging · BRICS-aligned carve-outs
  • India-flagged vessel cluster coordinated transit 18 May; bilateral carve-out with Iran confirmed (Tier 1): Six India-flagged vessels transited inbound on May 18 as a coordinated cluster following bilateral engagement with Iran, indicating functional safe-passage arrangements outside the coalition framework (Windward, Tier 1). This is the first operational evidence of a bifurcated Strait: coal-flagged / BRICS-aligned tonnage is moving under bilateral safe-passage agreements while Western-flagged vessels face toll demands and sanctions-screening risk.
  • PGSA toll regime enables Chinese yuan + Bitcoin settlement; dark-fleet expansion implicit (Tier 1): Reported per-transit payments of up to $2 million settled in Chinese yuan and Bitcoin transfers to IRGC-linked wallets (Windward, Tier 1). Dark and gray fleets and BRICS-aligned tonnage are positioned to absorb toll premiums and continue moving (Windward, Tier 1). Substitution is not a new commodity source but a new control mechanism: vessels are routing through Iran-administered corridors rather than avoiding the Strait.
  • Iran-Oman bilateral negotiations ongoing; Oman rejected toll authority in principle (Tier 2): Iranian and Omani technical teams recently met in Oman to discuss a framework for secure transit through the strait. Expert-level discussions with Omani counterparts would continue next week (Business Standard, 18 May, Tier 2). Oman's stated position: no tolls. But PGSA is operational regardless, suggesting bilateral carve-outs (India-flagged, BRICS partners) may be negotiated outside Oman's framework.
Why this matters — Substitution in this crisis is not commodity switching (e.g., Australia LNG for Qatar). It is shipping-lane switching: BRICS-aligned and dark-fleet tonnage is organizing inside the PGSA toll framework while Western-flagged vessels face secondary-sanctions risk if they pay tolls or kinetic risk if they don't. This bifurcation is the operational signature of the regime shift from kinetic to administrative control. For supply-chain leaders: substitution routes now require compliance with PGSA permit system + toll payment + sanctions-screening for Western entities + yuan/crypto currency conversion.
Implication — Traditional substitution (rerouting cargo to alternative ports, shifting tonnage flags) is occurring, but it is now embedded within a state-administered toll and security regime. Supply-chain teams need to track: (1) which carriers have formal safe-passage agreements with Iran (India-flagged, likely Chinese), (2) which routes are PGSA-approved (Larak Island corridor per Windward), (3) secondary-sanctions risk exposure if toll payment is made. The bifurcation is operational and structural; it is not temporary. Planning horizons extend to Q4 2026 minimum.

Methodology

This brief uses a tiered signal-weighting framework. Only verified physical events from primary sources can move the Wave Intensity assessment. Trend movement requires confirmed change versus the prior 72 hours. The system continuously calibrates against outcomes — when predictions miss, the underlying heuristics are sharpened. The full operating ruleset is kept private to preserve analytical edge.

Tier 2 — Specialised commercial intelligence with multi-year operational track record across energy, petrochemicals, maritime, and metals desks.
