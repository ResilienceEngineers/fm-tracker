03

Cascade through downstream chains

Deepening · Wave 3 now visible in tertiary industries
  • Bunker fuel shortage (Type 4 Distribution, tertiary FM): The Iran war and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz have disrupted supplies of the fuel that powers much of the global shipping industry. Bunker fuel powers ships responsible for transporting around 80% of globally traded goods by sea. Experts warn that shortages could drive up shipping costs, raise consumer prices and affect businesses worldwide. The prolonged cutoff from major sources of the heavier crude oil needed for bunker fuel, like Iraq and Kuwait, will cause shortages.
  • EU gas storage stress entering summer injection season: Europe is heading into the critical summer injection season with storage levels that are anything but comfortable. As of May 4, 2026, EU underground gas storage sits at just 34.14% full (386.34 TWh). Europe's imports are still expected to rise overall in 2026, but the near-term squeeze means higher costs for storage refill and potential demand destruction in industry and power if prices spike. Seasonal peaks this summer could create localized shortfalls, especially as Europe and Asia compete head-to-head.
  • Refined product scarcity deepening (jet fuel, diesel, gasoline): Tightness is quickly spreading to product markets as global refinery activity has slowed. Global oil demand is now expected to contract by 2.4 mb/d y-o-y in 2Q26. The steepest losses are seen in the petrochemical sector where feedstock availability is becoming increasingly constrained. Aviation activity is also running well below normal levels, helping to ease some of the pressure on jet fuel prices.
Why this matters: Type 4 Distribution (bunker fuel shortage) + Type 3 Feedstock (naphtha, refined products) + Type 2 Shipping (Strait closure) now constitute a three-layer cascade. Wave 3 is no longer confined to upstream feedstock; it is propagating into final-mile logistics and end-user demand. Bunker fuel shortage is visible in price (+60%), not yet in operator FM, because shipping companies are absorbing cost into freight surcharges. But if shortage persists into June, carriers will declare Type 4 FM on fuel supply, triggering rationing on container lines and shifting container traffic to air freight (which exacerbates jet fuel constraint). This is a cascade acceleration threshold.
Implication: Supply-chain executives should expect: (1) bunker fuel FM declaration from major carrier (MSC, Maersk, CMA CGM) within T+3–T+7; (2) container freight surcharges (currently 10–15%) rising to 25–30% by June 1; (3) shift to air freight pushing jet fuel prices further (jet already at near-$200/bbl); (4) EU summer gas injection falling short of target, triggering rotational demand destruction in industrial power by July. This is L4→L5 boundary condition: tertiary-industry FM declarations will follow if Strait remains effectively closed past 20 May.

Substitution / alternative sourcing

Accelerating · Atlantic Basin & non-Gulf diversification locked in
  • US LNG filling Qatar shortfall (confirmed by Edison, Petronet signals): Italian importer Edison already flagged disruption could stretch beyond mid-June, scrambling to replace 10 cancelled cargoes (about 1.4 billion cubic meters) with U.S. LNG. U.S. LNG exporters are the big winners here, ramping up utilization and filling the gap left by Qatar. North American natural gas producers will likely increase their share of liquefied natural gas going to Asia and Europe, with the U.S. likely to be a long-term beneficiary of this disruption.
  • China aluminium export growth (Type 5 Substitution signal): If China's aluminium exports reach up to 7.22 million tons by the end of 2026, which is 1.1 million tons higher than the volume in 2025, it will be close to offsetting the void created by Al Taweelah's non-operation for a year. But in case Alba and Qatalum continue to operate at a lower rate and the Strait of Hormuz remains under restrictions, who else will make up the deficit besides China? Asia already pivoting to Russian PFAs (Japan, South Korea auto-parts negotiations with RUSAL).
  • EGA rerouting through Oman (Sohar) + UAE Red Sea pipeline (Fujairah) + Arctic shipping exploration: EGA will reroute its aluminium exports and raw material imports through Oman's Sohar port, as the conflict has forced companies across the Gulf to adjust supply routes. Iran's chokehold on the Strait of Hormuz may be increasing the business argument for shipping by way of the Arctic, which is a harsh environment and requires specialized vessels and a costly icebreaker escort, but may start looking more attractive.
Why this matters: Substitution / alternative sourcing is the market's adaptive response to supply shock. US LNG + China aluminium + alternative ports (Sohar, Fujairah, Red Sea pipeline) are all Type 5 signals indicating the crisis is shifting from "shortage" to "reallocation." However, reallocation comes at cost (US LNG at premium, Chinese aluminium quality inconsistency for automotive, Sohar/Fujairah congestion, Arctic shipping capital intensity). These workarounds keep supply flowing but repricing global energy trade and shifting competitive advantage toward Atlantic Basin producers and China. This is structural, not temporary.
Implication: Diversification away from Gulf sourcing is now strategic priority, not contingency planning. Buyers locking in US LNG long-term contracts, auto-parts suppliers qualifying Russian / Chinese / Indian sources, and logistics operators investing in Red Sea / Arctic routing. This repricing is permanent even if Strait reopens by June. Expect: (1) QatarEnergy long-term contract prices 15–20% above pre-war levels through 2027; (2) Atlantic Basin crude premiums embedded; (3) alternative sourcing agreements announced Q2 2026 by major buyers (Europe, Asia, automotive OEMs). This marks transition from crisis-response to structural rebalancing.

Methodology

This brief uses a tiered signal-weighting framework. Only verified physical events from primary sources can move the Wave Intensity assessment. Trend movement requires confirmed change versus the prior 72 hours. The system continuously calibrates against outcomes — when predictions miss, the underlying heuristics are sharpened. The full operating ruleset is kept private to preserve analytical edge.

Sources

Inputs are graded across six tiers before they can move the assessment. Specific outlets are not published.

Tier 1 — Primary, official, verifiable: operator press releases and FM letters; stock-exchange filings; regulator notices; sovereign and central-bank communications; AIS-confirmed vessel movements.
Tier 2 — Specialised commercial intelligence with multi-year operational track record across energy, petrochemicals, maritime, and metals desks.
Tier 3 — Analytical institutions and multilateral commodity desks with rigorous publication standards.
Tier 4 — Commercial analysts and named columnists with auditable forecast records.
Tier 5 — Wire services and reputable regional / sector press.
Tier 6 — Excluded by default: anonymous OSINT, op-eds, and AI-summarised aggregator output. Admitted only with independent Tier 1–3 corroboration.
