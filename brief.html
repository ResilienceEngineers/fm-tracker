Kinetic / facility damage

Escalate · shipping attacks resuming · May 5–7
  • On 5 May, the CMA CGM San Antonio was reportedly struck by a cruise missile in the Strait of Hormuz, injuring eight crew members. Tier 1 (CENTCOM, shipping data). First kinetic damage to a commercial container vessel since April ceasefire. Proof of resumed attack capability.
  • On 7 May, Caixin reported that the Chinese chemical tanker JV Innovation was attacked, marking the first time a Chinese-owned vessel was targeted during the crisis. The vessel reported a fire on board to other vessels near Mina Saqr, UAE, on 4 May. Tier 1 (AIS data, Caixin). Escalation of target scope to Chinese flag vessels (prior focus on US/allied).
  • No facility-tier kinetic damage (e.g., refinery, LNG plant, smelter) reported in last 72h. All major facility FMs remain pre-May events (EGA March 28, ALBA March 28, Ras Laffan Feb 28). Kinetic risk is operational (vessel strikes), not structural.
Why this matters: Ceasefire framework (April 8) has degraded to selective attack window. CMA CGM San Antonio hit signals that Iranian/IRGC capability is intact and rules-of-engagement under ceasefire have shifted to target commercial traffic without blanket immunity. Chinese tanker fire extends target scope beyond US/Western flagged vessels. Risk profile for transits is hardening, not softening. This explains why single May 9 Qatar LNG transit does not translate to sustained traffic recovery—vessel operators are assessing hit probability on a per-voyage basis.
Implication: Ceasefire is not equivalent to reopening. Transit risk premium remains extreme. War-risk insurance and IRGC pre-clearance coordination impose transaction costs that eliminate marginal voyages and extend voyage planning timelines from days to weeks. This keeps chokepoint effective closure intact even if technical blockade is nominally eased. Expected cascade: risk-averse shipowners extend waiting/anchoring (holding pattern now 1,550+ vessels per CENTCOM); port congestion multiplies; bunker fuel rationing deepens because fewer transits = less refueling throughput = physical supply tightness.

03

Cascade through downstream chains

Escalate · Wave 3 distribution tier entry · bunker fuel + aviation
  • Bunker fuel shortage is the first explicit distribution-tier cascade signal. Ship operators rely on a sludgelike substance known as bunker fuel to keep vessels running. The Iran war's closure of the Strait of Hormuz has choked off the supply of this fuel that powers the global maritime industry and its largest refueling hub in Asia. Experts say that means a shortage of bunker fuel will translate to higher shipping costs, increase consumer prices and hurt the bottom lines of businesses worldwide. Tier 1 (AP/Reuters, May 12). This is Wave Type 4 (Distribution).
  • Jet fuel aviation cuts: Lufthansa says 20,000 short-haul flights would be removed from its schedule between now and October as the Iran war drives up oil prices and deepens worries that some countries may run low on jet fuel. Tier 1 (airline announcement). KLM is also canceling 160 intra-European flights, Scandinavian Airlines (SAS) has cut 1,000 April services, and Air Canada is removing 4 daily Toronto to New York flights serving John F. Kennedy International Airport from 1 June 2026. Type 4 (Distribution). First tertiary industry (passenger aviation) entering forced capacity contraction.
  • Aluminium long-dated contract weakness: The deeper signal sits in the longer-dated contracts. December 2027 bid and offer prices both fell 0.22%, settling at USD 3,118 per tonne and USD 3,123 per tonne respectively. This divergence between strengthening near-term prices and weakening long-dated contracts is not incidental. When spot prices rise while contracts dated 18 months forward decline, the market is not expressing confidence in a demand surge. Tier 2 (LME data, May 8). Type 3 (Downstream feedstock cascade). Market is pricing multi-year shortage, not recovery.
Why this matters: Wave 3 is now visible across three distinct cascade pathways: (1) maritime services (bunker fuel rationing), (2) passenger aviation (capacity cuts), (3) industrial pricing (aluminium long-dated bearishness). These are NOT naphtha-feedstock shortages (Wave 3 from Day 70). These are demand-destruction and logistics-cost amplifiers layering on top of upstream production loss. The cascade is broadening horizontally (more industries affected) and deepening vertically (from feedstock to services to demand).
Implication: Demand destruction is becoming a primary mitigation mechanism. Airlines cut capacity to ration fuel; shipping slows to conserve bunker; aluminium buyers de-stock because long-term supply outlook is grim. This flattens spot price pressure (less demand = less bidding war) but locks in multi-quarter low throughput (structural reduction, not cyclical adjustment). Supply chain equilibrium is shifting toward smaller global trade volumes at sustained elevated prices. Container shipping rates, which depend on port efficiency and fuel availability, are entering step-function cost increase. This hits exporters from Asia, Africa, and Eastern Europe disproportionately.

Substitution / alternative sourcing

Same · limited alternatives materializing · demand destruction primary mitigation
  • Jet fuel: Around 75 per cent of Europe's jet fuel imports come from the region, making any prolonged disruption especially challenging. Tier 2 (industry analysis). US jet fuel exports are rising but insufficient to offset loss of Middle Eastern throughput. Alternative: Airlines cut capacity rather than source substitutes. Demand destruction is cheaper than sourcing.
  • Aluminium: If China's aluminium exports manage to reach up to 7.22 million tons by the end of 2026, which is 1.1 million tons higher than the volume in 2025, then it will be close to offsetting the void to be created by Al Taweelah's non-operation for a year. Tier 2 (analyst estimate, May 2026). Chinese exports can offset ~35% of EGA Al Taweelah loss. Gap remains 65% (1.6 Mt actual vs 1.1 Mt available substitute).
  • Naphtha: Naphtha supply between May and July would recover to more than 90 percent of pre-war levels, with operating rates at naphtha cracking center facilities also expected to rebound above 90 percent. Tier 2 (South Korea Ministry of Trade estimate, May 2026). South Korean projections for naphtha supply recovery are optimistic (90%+). Implementation depends on Strait easing or substitution pipelines ramping. No confirmation yet.
Why this matters: Substitution is available but constrained. Chinese aluminium can offset ~35% of Gulf loss; US jet fuel can supplement European needs but at high cost premium; naphtha pipeline alternatives are limited by infrastructure and crude-grade compatibility. The supply chain is not choosing substitution as primary mitigation; instead, it is choosing capacity reduction (airlines cutting flights, shippers rationing fuel, converters running lower utilization). This suggests elasticity is low—most demand cannot actually shift to alternatives, so volumes contract instead.
Implication: Demand destruction is the market's revealed preference over substitution. This is structurally deflationary in the short term (less economic activity) but inflationary in the medium term (fewer producers, higher margins on surviving capacity, reduced competition). By Q3 2026, expect consolidation in aviation (smaller carriers exit), shipping (independent operators fold into alliances), and downstream conversion (smaller margins force plant closures in Europe/Asia). Substitution that might have been viable in a sustained-closure scenario is being bypassed for simpler capacity cuts. This accelerates the formation of supply-chain bifurcation: integrated operators (Shell, TotalEnergies, Saudi Aramco) survive; independents (small refiners, single-asset operators) do not.

Middle path (B, ceasefire holds, traffic normalizes) has degraded to 30% because observed kinetic risk (shipping strikes) conflicts with diplomatic de-escalation narrative. The market is pricing in structural change (either prolonged low-throughput or renewed conflict) over return to status-quo-ante.

Scenario B (normalization by mid-June) is now a tail-risk upside case. Risk managers should tier their contingency plans: (1) Core assumption = A (sustained closure, demand destruction); (2) Downside = C (escalation); (3) Upside = B (ceasefire holds). Methodology

This brief uses a tiered signal-weighting framework. Only verified physical events from primary sources can move the Wave Intensity assessment. Trend movement requires confirmed change versus the prior 72 hours. The system continuously calibrates against outcomes — when predictions miss, the underlying heuristics are sharpened. The full operating ruleset is kept private to preserve analytical edge.

Sources

Inputs are graded across six tiers before they can move the assessment. Specific outlets are not published.

Tier 1 — Primary, official, verifiable: operator press releases and FM letters; stock-exchange filings; regulator notices; sovereign and central-bank communications; AIS-confirmed vessel movements.
Tier 2 — Specialised commercial intelligence with multi-year operational track record across energy, petrochemicals, maritime, and metals desks.
Tier 3 — Analytical institutions and multilateral commodity desks with rigorous publication standards.
Tier 4 — Commercial analysts and named columnists with auditable forecast records.
Tier 5 — Wire services and reputable regional / sector press.
Tier 6 — Excluded by default: anonymous OSINT, op-eds, and AI-summarised aggregator output. Admitted only with independent Tier 1–3 corroboration.
