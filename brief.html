Cascade through downstream chains

Bunker shortage now Tier 1 · Type 4 formal
  • Bunker fuel Type 4 Distribution shortage confirmed Tier 1 (12 May): Euronews, AGBI, OilPrice, AP. Singapore prices $800/tonne (+60% from $500 pre-conflict). Experts cite 80% of global seaborne trade relies on bunker fuel. Clarksons Research reported that average speed of bulk carriers and container ships has fallen by about 2% since Feb. 28 as operators reduce fuel use by slowing voyages. Transport and Environment has estimated the crisis is costing the global shipping industry about 340 million euros ($400 million) per day. Type 4 emerging from analyst commentary (Soft) into price + behavioural data (Hard).
  • Lotte Chemical restart delay +11 days (Tier 2, 18 May): Lee held an on-site roundtable with representatives of LG Chem, Lotte Chemical, Hanwha TotalEnergies Petrochemical and Hyundai Chemical. Seoul government March 26 imposed naphtha export controls (Tier 1, Korea Herald). Delay from May 18 announced to May 29 actual signals feedstock scarcity persists; no alleviating shipments from Middle East. Cascade from Type 1 (QE production halt) → Type 3 (naphtha shipping) → Type 1 converter (Lotte) now 81 days post-anchor.
  • Aluminium contract cascade deepening (Tier 1, Wood Mackenzie May 2026): Wood Mackenzie said the Middle East conflict is triggering a critical supply crisis in the global aluminium market, with disruptions potentially removing 3 to 3.5 million tonnes of output in 2026. Global deficit through 2026 embeds no mid-year recovery. Downstream: automotive (EV batteries, structural), packaging, aerostructure all face price volatility and allocation.
Why this matters — Bunker fuel emergence as a formal Tier 1 shortage (not just price spike) is the key Wave 3 progression. When shipping becomes supply-constrained and explicitly cost-burdened ($400m/day per Transport & Environment), the cascade multiplier scales: every day of Hormuz closure now adds 10–30 bps to freight rates globally. Slow-steaming (2% speed reduction, Clarksons) is an operational adaption that buys time but does not restore throughput; it delays cargo arrival and inflates in-transit inventory, a secondary cascade effect. Lotte's +11-day delay is Tier 2 but corroborated by Korea's naphtha controls (Tier 1), so it reads as reliable signal of persistent shortage. Aluminium deficit 3–3.5 Mt is 4–5% of global primary output; combined with EGA/Alba offline timeline and China's 45 Mt capacity cap, the market is facing a structural rebalancing that will persist through Q4 2026.
Implication — Type 4 (Distribution) FM signals are accumulating but not yet formalized by maritime operators. This is the boundary between L4 Systemic and L5 Regime. If shipping (Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM) declare FM in the next 48–72h due to bunker shortages, that would be the primary-route escalation to L5. For now, L4 Systemic stands because Type 4 FM threshold has not been crossed by an A-list operator. The 340m/day cost of the crisis is internalized by the shipping industry (absorbed in rate increases, not yet FM language). Watch for: mid-May earnings releases from Maersk, MSC Gülsüm, or tramp operators citing force majeure on fuel availability; second watch is whether bunker prices spike above $900/tonne or inventory levels in Singapore fall below 30-day cover.

  • Mozambique LNG restart FM lifted (Tier 1, TotalEnergies, January 2026): This restart of project activities onshore and offshore follows the decision made on 7 November 2025, by Mozambique LNG consortium to lift the Force Majeure that was declared in 2021 and resume project activities. Why this matters — Substitution signals are sparse and tactical, not strategic. US LNG export growth is real (0.9 Bcf/d added April alone) but faces its own constraints: limited feedstock in Henry Hub environment, new train ramp cycles (Golden Pass Train 1 at startup, Corpus Christi Stage 3 ramps through H2 2026). Korea's naphtha lock-in (export controls) is a government-level substitution barrier: domestic cracker run rates are constrained by feedstock availability, not demand. Lotte's 11-day delay is a symptom of this lock-in. No announcements of strategic rerouting (e.g., Korea securing 5-year spot allocations from Russian refineries, LG Chem locking African crude imports) suggest supply chains are still in crisis mode, not deal-making mode.
Implication — Absence of strategic substitution contracts through May 19 is consistent with L4 Systemic (not L5 Regime). In L5, we would expect: (1) long-term off-take agreements announced (e.g., Japan signing 3-year LNG with Australia beyond current spot), (2) government-level supply rerouting (India securing Russian crude pipeline alternative), (3) refinery/cracker capacity M&A or joint venture announcements. None of these have emerged in the 81 days since Day 1. Instead, the market is absorbing via: spot price spikes (bunker $800, naphtha highs), operational adjustments (slow-steaming, run-rate cuts), and government controls (Korea export ban). This is consistent with L4, where supply remains tight but not yet forcing permanent structural shifts. Watch for: announcements of long-term LNG allocation renegotiations (Korea, Italy, Belgium, China) by end of May; any sovereign-level deal signing (e.g., Saudi Aramco to new European buyers) mid-June; capacity additions (US, Australia) ramping through June.

Methodology

This brief uses a tiered signal-weighting framework. Only verified physical events from primary sources can move the Wave Intensity assessment. Trend movement requires confirmed change versus the prior 72 hours. The system continuously calibrates against outcomes — when predictions miss, the underlying heuristics are sharpened. The full operating ruleset is kept private to preserve analytical edge.

Sources

Inputs are graded across six tiers before they can move the assessment. Specific outlets are not published.

Tier 1 — Primary, official, verifiable: operator press releases and FM letters; stock-exchange filings; regulator notices; sovereign and central-bank communications; AIS-confirmed vessel movements.
Tier 2 — Specialised commercial intelligence with multi-year operational track record across energy, petrochemicals, maritime, and metals desks.
Tier 3 — Analytical institutions and multilateral commodity desks with rigorous publication standards.
Tier 4 — Commercial analysts and named columnists with auditable forecast records.
Tier 5 — Wire services and reputable regional / sector press.
Tier 6 — Excluded by default: anonymous OSINT, op-eds, and AI-summarised aggregator output. Admitted only with independent Tier 1–3 corroboration.
