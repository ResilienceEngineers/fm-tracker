Iran is moving to formalize a state-administered transit-toll regime under the new Persian Gulf Strait Authority (PGSA), with reported per-transit payments of up to $2 million settled in Chinese yuan and Bitcoin transfers to IRGC-linked wallets. Cascade Through Downstream Chains

Wave 3 cascade depth increasing
  • Bunker fuel shortage (Singapore): $800/tonne confirmed. The Iran war's closure of the Strait of Hormuz has choked off the supply of this fuel that powers the global maritime industry and its largest refueling hub in Asia. That will be an issue first in Asia, which relies heavily on Middle Eastern oil. In Singapore, the world's biggest refueling hub for bunker fuel, reserves are dwindling and prices are spiking. Type 4 Distribution FM not yet filed by any shipping operator, but commercial signal strong (Tier 2, Industry). Cascade vector: shipping cost inflation → container rate increases → downstream inflation.
  • Ethylene glycol / PET packaging: Naphtha FM propagation ongoing. Lotte Chemical halted ethylene production capacity of 1.23 million tons at its plant in the Yeosu National Industrial Complex on the 27th; the successive closures of naphtha cracking centers are expected to worsen supply shortages of everyday products such as plastics and vinyl. Restart 29 May on track; no new FM filed 19–22 May, but suppliers report allocation rationing continuing downstream.
  • Aviation fuel / jet fuel scarcity signals (Lufthansa ongoing route suspensions). Lufthansa Group routes to Middle East remain suspended (confirmed via Day 81 brief). While there are more than 890 LNG-fueled vessels in operation globally, a lack of supporting infrastructure has created bottlenecks for them. But the industry is catching up and limits on bunker fuel are driving even more interest in LNG-capable ships.
Why this matters. Wave 3 deepening is characterized by spreading across uncorrelated supply chains. Bunker fuel (maritime energy input), ethylene (polymer input), and aviation fuel (infrastructure input) are not directly linked to the Strait closure or LNG/naphtha supply paths individually—but they all compete for refining capacity and feedstock allocation. The cascade vector is now the shipping cost + fuel cost dual transmission mechanism.
Implication. Downstream industries (packaging, automotive, retail, pharma logistics) face a two-layer cost squeeze: (i) raw material allocation (naphtha/ethylene glycol shortages persist through 29 May restart window), and (ii) freight cost inflation (bunker fuel $800/tonne vs. $500 pre-crisis = 60% cost adder on every shipment ex-Asia). This is a margin-compression environment that will persist through Q3 2026 if the Strait remains under administrative control.

  • SABIC Tadawul "cannot estimate" filing (Day 41): no update 19–22 May. KPC FM#2 "even when reopens" language signals permanent forward-looking constraint, not temporary outage. Both are long-duration FMs that will extend restart estimates.
Why this matters. Restart signals have inverted meaning. Pre-crisis, a "restart" signal meant "this facility will be back to normal capacity within N weeks." Now it means "this operator has strategically selected which supply chains to prioritize and which to rationally constrain." Lotte's choice to stage medical supplies and infrastructure admixtures first reflects both ethical prioritization and commercial realism: pharma and construction are less price-sensitive than commodity polymers, so supply is allocated to higher-margin chains first.
Implication. Restart-type FMs are now the most durable signal of structural supply constraint, not temporary relief. Commodity plastics, base chemicals, and fertilizers will see rationing through Q3 2026 even after facility restarts, because operators will prioritize high-margin specialty and essential-use chains. Procurement executives should identify substitutable commodity inputs now; substitution will be cheaper than allocation delays.

Substitution / Alternative Sourcing

Bifurcation deepening: BRICS + dark-fleet absorption
  • Dark and gray fleets absorbing toll premiums. Dark and gray fleets and BRICS-aligned tonnage are positioned to absorb toll premiums and continue moving. Western-aligned tonnage faces a compounding compliance dilemma: payment exposes vessels to OFAC secondary-sanctions risk, while non-payment exposes them to IRGC interdiction. Dark and gray fleets and BRICS-aligned tonnage are positioned to absorb toll premiums and continue moving.
  • Alternative fuel infrastructure (LNG-capable ships) gaining adoption. About a third of ships that the company is managing the construction of will be "dual fuel capable," meaning they can run on both conventional bunker fuel and alternatives such as liquefied natural gas. Ship owners are willing to pay a premium to have vessels that can switch between fuels because "in a volatile environment optionality has a measurable economic value."
  • Saudi Aramco LNG substitution strategy. In early 2026, Saudi Aramco signed a 20-year deal to receive 1 million tonnes of liquefied natural gas per year from USA. This long-term contract is a substitution signal: Saudi pivot away from Gulf-sourced gas toward US supply, de facto accepting permanent Strait constraint as a business model.
Why this matters. Substitution is not a temporary workaround; it is becoming the baseline. BRICS-aligned and non-Western suppliers have structural advantages in the new Strait regime: they do not face secondary-sanctions risk from toll payment; their currencies (RMB, INR, AED) are acceptable to Iran; their vessels can operate under false flags more easily. Western shipping networks are becoming less competitive in the Middle East trade at the margin. This bifurcation will persist even if ceasefire talks succeed, because the economic advantages are structural, not reversible.
Implication. Long-term sourcing strategies must account for a bifurcated world. EU and US importers of crude, LNG, and naphtha will face a 10–20% structural cost premium vs. BRICS buyers if the Strait toll regime becomes permanent (expected probability: 35–40%). Procurement should explore new routes (Suez for some Africa-Asia flows, Cape for others) and alternative suppliers (US LNG, Australian alumina, North Sea naphtha) now, while competition for alternative capacity is still moderate. Decision window closes by end of Q3 2026.

Outlook Scenarios

T+30 horizon; three paths to mid-June reset

Scenario A: Negotiated Opening (35% probability). By 20 June, Trump and Iran reach agreement on Strait access terms. PGSA toll regime becomes semi-permanent; Strait opens to commercial traffic at $500K–$1M per transit (formally styled "specialized services fee"). BRICS and non-sanctioned vessels clear first; Western tankers follow within 2–3 weeks. Restart-type FMs lift (QE, SABIC, KPC timelines firm up). Wave 3 cascade effects plateau; wholesale price pressures ease by end of Q2. Oil price $100–110 range. Probability driver: Trump "no hurry" signal suggests willingness to negotiate terms, not timeline.

Scenario B: Stalemate (40% probability). Ceasefire extends indefinitely; negotiations stall on nuclear and regional proxy terms. PGSA becomes de facto permanent; Strait remains ~2–10 transits/day. US naval blockade of Iran continues. Dark-fleet economics absorb $2M toll payments; sanctioned entities face secondary-sanctions risk but vessel insurance covers losses. Commodity prices plateau at current elevated levels; no normalization. Restart-type FMs extend into Q4 2026; long-term contracts default to BRICS suppliers. Wave 3 cascade effects become structural (supply-chain regionalization). Probability driver: Trump 20 May statement that he has "time" and historical precedent (12 years Iraq, 19 years Vietnam).

Scenario C: Escalation (25% probability). Trump orders new kinetic operations in Strait or Iran mainland by 15 June. IRGC retaliates; Strait closure becomes kinetic again (mines, vessel strikes). US Navy undertakes mine-clearing operations; direct combat resumes. Oil price spikes $130–180/bbl. Restart-type FMs extend indefinitely; Wave 4 cascade (default cascades, credit-line freezes, regional supply-chain collapse) becomes operational. Probability driver: Trump's 20 May language ("do we finish it up or do they sign") and ongoing shadow drone war suggest escalation risk remains present if negotiations plateau further.

Methodology

This brief uses a tiered signal-weighting framework. Only verified physical events from primary sources can move the Wave Intensity assessment. Trend movement requires confirmed change versus the prior 72 hours. The system continuously calibrates against outcomes — when predictions miss, the underlying heuristics are sharpened. The full operating ruleset is kept private to preserve analytical edge.

Sources

Inputs are graded across six tiers before they can move the assessment. Specific outlets are not published.

Tier 1 — Primary, official, verifiable: operator press releases and FM letters; stock-exchange filings; regulator notices; sovereign and central-bank communications; AIS-confirmed vessel movements.
Tier 2 — Specialised commercial intelligence with multi-year operational track record across energy, petrochemicals, maritime, and metals desks.
Tier 3 — Analytical institutions and multilateral commodity desks with rigorous publication standards.
Tier 4 — Commercial analysts and named columnists with auditable forecast records.
Tier 5 — Wire services and reputable regional / sector press.
Tier 6 — Excluded by default: anonymous OSINT, op-eds, and AI-summarised aggregator output. Admitted only with independent Tier 1–3 corroboration.
