Kinetic / facility damage

Alba & EGA strike damage cascade continues
  • Alba damage assessment ongoing. Alba initiated a "controlled and safe shutdown" of about 19 per cent of its production capacity affecting lines 1, 2 and 3, which contribute to Alba's total production capacity of more than 1.62 million metric tonnes per annum. Damage from March 29 strikes continues to constrain output; no restart timeline announced.
  • EGA Al Taweelah uncontrolled shutdown. Emirates Global Aluminium halted operations at its Al Taweelah smelter after the site was struck; the smelter lost power due to the strikes and smelting facilities were forced into an uncontrolled shutdown; metal has solidified inside the smelting circuits, causing significant damage. 12-month rebuild timeline (April 9, Bloomberg, Tier 2).
  • Qatalum capacity reduction. Qatalum maintaining aluminium production at 60 per cent of its capacity. Gas shortage driver (QatarEnergy LNG cut); not directly damage-related, but capacity loss quantified at ~40% offline.
Why this matters The three largest Gulf aluminium producers (EGA, Alba, Qatalum) account for ~8–9% of global supply. Damage to smelting circuits (potlines, electrical systems) translates to multi-month offline windows. Wood Mackenzie predicts a market deficit of 4 Mt in 2026 and a reduction of global output by 3%. This is no longer a logistics issue; it is a production capacity loss that will persist into Q4 2026 and beyond.
Implication Aluminium cascade is Wave 3 physical absence, not Wave 2 allocation. EGA's 12-month rebuild + Alba 19% offline + Qatalum 40% offline means the GCC loses ~3.5 Mt of annual capacity through 2026. Downstream users (automotive, beverage, aerospace) will see price escalation and contract volumes shrink. US manufacturers, which depend 85% on imports, face a strategic vulnerability if Gulf production does not recover by Q1 2027.
Sources · Alba (official statements) · EGA (official statements) · Bloomberg (April 9, EGA rebuild timeline) · Wood Mackenzie (April, market deficit forecast) · Tier 1–2.

  • Naphtha feedstock tightness cascades to crackers. Sadara stated that it "cannot provide, at the present time, an estimate for the return to production, as this is contingent on domestic and international factors." Lotte 11-day restart delay (May 18 → 29) is downstream signal of reduced refining. Maybank June halt risk flag confirms multi-week feedstock gap expected to persist.
Why this matters Wave 3 cascade is now measurable across three distinct tertiary chains: bunker (maritime shipping cost multiplier), aviation (crew hours, fuel surcharge), and naphtha (cracker utilization). These are not operator-level FM declarations, but they are economically material. A shortage of bunker fuel will translate to higher shipping costs, increase consumer prices and hurt the bottom lines of businesses worldwide. Bunker shortage is the leading indicator of L5 Regime transition if maritime operators begin formalizing FM.
Implication Tertiary supply chains are decoupling from primary operator control. Bunker fuel, naphtha, and aviation are now independently constrained by Strait closure and refining interruption, not just by QatarEnergy FM or EGA damage. If bunker FM is declared by MSC, Maersk, or CMA within 7 days, Wave 3 cascade will be formalized as industry-level disruption (L5). Multi-quarter duration expected; container shipping rates and port dwell times will remain elevated through Q3 2026.

Absence of new restart-type FMs in 72h suggests stabilization, not recovery.
Implication The restart-type FM cohort (QE, KPC, SABIC, EGA) now embodies the fundamental constraint: supply capacity is not recoverable until the Strait is (a) physically cleared, and (b) Iranian naval operations cease or are sufficiently contained. No material restart signal until after Strait reopens AND at least 30 days of safe commercial transit is confirmed.

Substitution / alternative sourcing

China & Russia emerging as secondary sources
  • Aluminium: China substitution ramping. Kiki Xi, an analyst at consultancy Aize China, said: "It's difficult for the aluminium production lines that sustained damage to recover in the short term, and that will likely lead some orders to flow to China"; Fastmarkets has revised its projections for Chinese aluminium exports, expanding to 12-18 per cent compared to the prior outlook of potentially flat to slightly negative. Japanese auto-parts makers negotiating with RUSAL for foundry alloys (Tier 2, April sources).
  • LNG: US Gulf and Canada ramping. Much of that demand has shifted to the US Gulf Coast, where new capacity from terminals such as Plaquemines, Corpus Christi and Sabine Pass has helped cushion the global supply shock; LNG Canada has also begun contributing volumes after its first cargoes in 2025. Europe seeing 20% price fall in response to US LNG avail signals (Tier 2, Tufts, May).
  • Crude: Saudi reroute via Yanbu & Red Sea. Nearly half of what would have been shipped is instead being transported daily through pipelines across Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates; but the rest of the oil is not being delivered. Capacity limited to ~10 Mt/yr; cannot substitute for lost Qatar LNG or Gulf refining.
Why this matters Substitution is real but capacity-constrained. China aluminium + US LNG + Saudi pipelines together offset ~30–40% of lost Gulf supply. However, these are high-cost alternatives: If Alba and Qatalum continue to operate at a lower rate and the Strait of Hormuz remains under restrictions, then who else will make up the deficit besides China? Russia aluminium and North America LNG are the swing sources, but they operate under geopolitical constraints (sanctions on Russia, limited US capacity expansion until 2027).
Implication Substitution is a 2–3 quarter relief valve, not a structural fix. Supply elasticity in aluminium, LNG, and crude is limited by existing capacity + time-to-build (new smelters require 2–3 years; LNG trains require 1–2 years; cracking units require 18 months). If Strait closure extends past Q3 2026, price escalation will accelerate because substitution capacity is exhausted. Supply-chain executives should lock in medium-term contracts (6–12 months) with US LNG and Chinese aluminium providers by June 2026 to avoid Q3 shortage escalation.
Sources · Fastmarkets (April, aluminium export revision) · RUSAL (anecdotal auto-parts outreach, Tokyo/Seoul sources) · Plaquemines Authority / Cheniere / Corpus Christi (LNG capacity statements, Tier 2) · Saudi Energy Ministry (Yanbu reroute, Tier 1) · Tufts Fletcher School (May, Weitz analysis, Tier 2).

Outlook scenarios

Three-quarter horizon; 70/20/10 split
  • Scenario A: Strait remains functionally closed through Q3 2026; no primary restarts; L4 Systemic holds. As of early May 2026, the Strait has been effectively closed to commercial shipping for 69 or more days. Iran maintains IRGC blockade; QE, KPC, EGA, SABIC remain offline. Bunker shortage evolves into formal maritime operator FM (MSC, Maersk, CMA). Tertiary supply-chain costs (bunker, naphtha, jet fuel) escalate 15–25% by end of Q2 2026. L4 Wave Intensity holds; L5 boundary approached but not crossed (pending maritime FM formalization). Probability: 70%.
  • Scenario B: Partial Strait reopening by 30 June; KPC or SABIC restart declared; L4 transitions to L3 Elevated. Even after Iran and the United States announced a ceasefire on 8 April, ship traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remained far below pre-war levels. A new diplomatic initiative or military de-escalation allows limited transits (50–70% pre-war volume); one primary operator formalizes restart. Restarting all 12 operable trains at Ras Laffan could take until the end of August if the process began in early May; restarting just the North site, with a capacity of 41 million tpa, would take just over a month. Bunker shortage eases; secondary operators (Lotte, Evonik, Braskem) restart by mid-July. L4→L3 transition by end of Q2. Probability: 20%.
  • Scenario C: Strait closure extends to end of Q3 2026; all major operators issue extended multi-year FMs; L4 escalates to L5 Regime. Iran-US military escalation resumes; Strait remains closed or intermittently accessible. KPC, SABIC, Qatalum, EGA all declare multi-year FMs (2027 restart language). The shipping crisis in the Strait of Hormuz is now "the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market", according to the head of the International Energy Agency, Fatih Birol. Global GDP growth revised downward 0.5–1.0% for 2026. Supply-chain price escalation exceeds 30–40%; several global manufacturers announce capacity shifts to North America or Southeast Asia. L5 Regime confirmed by end of Q3. Probability: 10%.
Why these scenarios matter Scenario A (70%) is the baseline: no material escalation from Day 78 conditions. Scenario B (20%) requires a geopolitical inflection (diplomatic breakthrough or US strategic decision to pause military operations). Scenario C (10%) requires a second military escalation or sustained Iranian blockade hardening. All three assume Hormuz remains the bottleneck; no scenario assumes non-Hormuz alternative routes (Yanbu pipeline, Red Sea via Suez) become primary. Supply planning must assume Scenario A as baseline case through June 2026.
Implication Strategy for supply-chain executives: (1) Execute long-term contracts with US LNG (Plaquemines, Sabine, Corpus Christi) and Canadian suppliers by end of June for 12-month forward buys. (2) Monitor KPC and SABIC filings weekly for any extension of "cannot estimate" language past their current June / July windows — this is the L4→L5 tripwire. (3) Prepare for bunker fuel FM declaration from MSC or Maersk within 7 days as second L4→L5 signal. (4) Assume Q3 supply deficit; plan inventory builds now (cost ~1.5–2% of procurement budget, but insurance against Scenario C). (5) Avoid spot-market purchasing for critical feedstocks (naphtha, LNG, methanol, helium) — contract forward with premium pricing acceptable vs. stockout risk.

Methodology

This brief uses a tiered signal-weighting framework. Only verified physical events from primary sources can move the Wave Intensity assessment. Trend movement requires confirmed change versus the prior 72 hours. The system continuously calibrates against outcomes — when predictions miss, the underlying heuristics are sharpened. The full operating ruleset is kept private to preserve analytical edge.
